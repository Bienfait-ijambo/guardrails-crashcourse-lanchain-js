# QUIBI: THE $1.75 BILLION MISTAKE
**Documentary script · Dark business storytelling · Approx. 7–9 minutes, depending on delivery**


Now I would rewrite it to start with the bizarre collapse, the $1.75 billion gamble, and the mystery of how it all went wrong. The original opening spends too much time explaining the business before giving viewers a reason to care.

## Revised hook — Quibi's spectacular collapse

NARRATOR — dark, fast, slightly cynical:

Hollywood gave them the money. The biggest celebrities gave them the star power. And they had $1.75 BILLION to reinvent television.

But in just six months, it was OVER.

[BEAT — MUSIC CUTS]

A company backed by some of the most powerful people in entertainment had managed to build a billion-dollar streaming service that almost nobody wanted to keep paying for.

And the strangest part?

They weren't trying to make movies longer. They were trying to make Hollywood fit into ten-minute bites on your phone.

[VISUAL: A Hollywood red carpet abruptly cuts to a lonely smartphone screen. The Quibi logo flickers.]

They called it Quibi.

And its story is packed with bizarre decisions, expensive bets, a disastrous launch, and one question that still makes its collapse worth examining:

How do you raise $1.75 billion to build the future of entertainment... and watch the whole thing fall apart before the year is even over?

From Hollywood's grand ambitions to one of streaming's most spectacular shutdowns, this is the story of Quibi.

# ACT I — THE PEOPLE WHO THOUGHT THEY COULD CHANGE HOLLYWOOD

*[VISUAL: Archival footage of Jeffrey Katzenberg at Disney and DreamWorks. Cut to Meg Whitman at eBay and Hewlett-Packard. Slow, deliberate editing.]*

**NARRATOR:**

To understand why Quibi attracted so much money, you have to understand the people behind it.

Jeffrey Katzenberg wasn't some outsider hoping to get lucky in Hollywood. He had helped lead Disney during its animation renaissance, an era that included films like *The Lion King* and *Aladdin*. He later co-founded DreamWorks, helping build another major force in entertainment.

Katzenberg knew Hollywood. He knew celebrities. He knew how to get major studios to take a meeting.

And then there was Meg Whitman.

She had led eBay through a period of extraordinary growth and later became CEO of Hewlett-Packard. One had spent decades building entertainment franchises. The other had experience running enormous businesses.

Together, they had something investors found difficult to ignore: credibility.

*[VISUAL: Investor presentations, studio lots, scripts, production sets.]*

The entertainment industry was being reshaped. Netflix was changing how people watched television. YouTube had made online video part of everyday life. And smartphones were becoming the screen people carried everywhere.

Katzenberg believed there was a gap between those worlds.

What if you combined Hollywood-level production with the convenience of mobile video? Not amateur clips. Not hour-long episodes. Premium stories designed for the small gaps in people's day.

A ten-minute bus ride. A lunch break. A few minutes waiting in line.

The concept became Quibi — short for “quick bites.”

Investors bought into the vision, and the company raised $1.75 billion before launch.

But money and reputation can open doors. They cannot prove that customers want what's on the other side.

**And Quibi's first big challenge wasn't making Hollywood shows. It was figuring out why anyone would need a whole new service to watch them.**

*[RE-HOOK / TRANSITION: VISUAL: A huge production budget appears beside a tiny phone screen.]*

---

# ACT II — BUILDING A HOLLYWOOD EMPIRE BEFORE FINDING AN AUDIENCE

**NARRATOR — confident, almost admiring:**

Quibi wasn't going to compete with YouTube by making videos on a shoestring budget.

It was bringing Hollywood's biggest guns into short-form entertainment.

Steven Spielberg. Idris Elba. Liam Hemsworth. Chrissy Teigen.

The company assembled an impressive roster of talent and developed original thrillers, documentaries, reality programs, and comedy series.

The spending was extraordinary. Quibi's leadership discussed production budgets reaching as high as $100,000 per minute.

Think about that.

A platform built around episodes lasting ten minutes or less was willing to spend sums that made much of the online video world look like a completely different business.

*[VISUAL: Production costs climb on screen. The number $1,750,000,000 fills the frame.]*

But Quibi wasn't merely building a library of shows. It was betting that people would pay for a new kind of viewing habit.

Its design was mobile-first. One of its signature features was Turnstyle, which let viewers switch between portrait and landscape orientations while watching. The technology gave Quibi a distinctive identity: the show could adapt to how someone held their phone.

It was clever. It was expensive. It was different.

But different isn't automatically better — and clever technology doesn't automatically become a reason to subscribe.

Quibi had solved one problem: how to make premium shows fit a phone.

**But it hadn't solved the more dangerous problem: why would people choose these shows over the entertainment they already had for free?**

*[RE-HOOK / TRANSITION: VISUAL: A phone screen splits into YouTube, TikTok, Netflix, and Quibi.]*

---

# ACT III — THE TEN-MINUTE TRAP

*[VISUAL: A commuter on a train, someone waiting in a queue, a person taking a lunch break.]*

**NARRATOR — conversational, slightly ironic:**

On paper, the idea made sense.

People were busy. Their attention was fragmented. They had smartphones in their pockets and spare minutes scattered throughout the day.

So why not give them premium entertainment that fit those gaps?

Quibi wanted to own the moments between everything else.

But there was a trap hidden inside that simple pitch.

The company wasn't just betting that people wanted short videos. People were already watching short videos — constantly.

Quibi was betting that they would pay for a separate service to watch professionally produced short videos when free alternatives were fighting for the same attention.

On YouTube, people could watch creators they already loved. On TikTok, they could discover an endless stream of quick entertainment. On Netflix, they could settle in for films and full-length series.

Quibi asked consumers to download another app, learn another format, and potentially pay for another subscription.

That was a much harder sell than simply saying, “People are busy.”

And there was another assumption: that people would mostly want this entertainment on their phones, in those little gaps between activities.

**Quibi had built its business around a very specific moment in people's lives. Then, right before launch, the entire world was about to change what those moments looked like.**

*[RE-HOOK / TRANSITION: VISUAL: A calendar flips to March 2020. Empty streets. Lockdown headlines.]*

---

# ACT IV — THE WORLD CHANGES, AND QUIBI LAUNCHES ANYWAY

**NARRATOR — slow, ominous:**

On April 6, 2020, Quibi launched.

And the timing was almost cruel.

The COVID-19 pandemic was disrupting daily life. Cities were shutting down. Offices emptied. Commuters stayed home. The ordinary routines that had inspired Quibi's concept were being transformed almost overnight.

The train ride. The walk to work. The lunch break away from the office.

Suddenly, millions of people were spending far more time at home — where they already had televisions, laptops, and familiar streaming services.

The pandemic wasn't something Quibi could have predicted with certainty when developing its original concept. And mobile-first entertainment might still have found an audience under different circumstances.

But the launch experience created another problem.

At launch, Quibi did not support casting its shows to televisions. Users couldn't simply move the experience from their phone to the living-room TV. The company accelerated plans to add casting support after users complained.

*[VISUAL: A viewer looks at a phone, then at a large television. Pause.]*

**NARRATOR — dry:**

Imagine spending billions of dollars to reinvent television...

...and launching a service that initially makes watching on a television impossible.

*[BEAT.]*

That wasn't the only issue. The pandemic exposed a deeper vulnerability: Quibi's identity depended on a particular use case. When the world changed, the company had to persuade consumers that its product still belonged in their lives.

But a difficult launch doesn't automatically doom a company. A strong product can recover from bad timing, and a strong launch can turn curious downloads into loyal customers.

**The real test was what happened after people tried Quibi. Would they stay — and, more importantly, would they pay?**

*[RE-HOOK / TRANSITION: VISUAL: App-store ratings, download headlines, a subscription screen.]*

---

# ACT V — ATTENTION WASN'T THE SAME AS DEMAND

**NARRATOR:**

Quibi arrived with enormous expectations. But attention and adoption are not the same thing.

A person might download an app because the launch is getting attention. They might try a free trial because the shows look interesting. But the business only works if enough people keep watching and decide the service deserves a permanent place in their entertainment budget.

Those were much harder questions.

Quibi was competing in a crowded market, and its competitors had different advantages.

YouTube offered a huge range of free content. TikTok was built around rapid discovery and sharing. Netflix was already a destination for premium entertainment.

Quibi asked people to adopt a new app, learn a new format, and pay for another subscription. Its celebrity lineup generated attention, but attention wasn't the same as retention. Its viewing technology was distinctive, but technology couldn't create a reason to return by itself.

The uncomfortable possibility was that Quibi had correctly identified a real behavior — people watching short videos on their phones — but had misjudged the business opportunity hidden inside it.

Short-form video was popular. That didn't automatically mean consumers wanted another paid streaming service devoted to it.

And the more money Quibi had committed to its strategy, the more expensive that miscalculation became.

*[VISUAL: Quibi's logo appears beside competing apps. The other screens remain active as Quibi fades.]*

**But how long could a company with this much funding keep going if it couldn't turn its grand vision into a sustainable audience? The answer came far sooner than anyone behind Quibi wanted.**

*[RE-HOOK / TRANSITION: VISUAL: Calendar pages flip from April to October 2020. Music builds, then cuts.]*

---

# ACT VI — SIX MONTHS LATER

**NARRATOR — cold, measured:**

October 21, 2020.

Just over six months after launch, Quibi's founders announced that the company was winding down.

The platform that had raised $1.75 billion to reshape entertainment had failed to establish enough demand to sustain its ambitions.

The announcement was remarkable not simply because Quibi was closing. Startups fail all the time.

It was remarkable because of the scale of the bet, the experience of the people behind it, and how quickly the experiment had come to an end.

Katzenberg and Whitman acknowledged two fundamental possibilities: perhaps the idea wasn't strong enough to justify a standalone streaming service, or perhaps the timing was wrong.

*[VISUAL: Production sets empty out. Scripts are stacked. The Quibi app icon disappears from a phone.]*

But the shutdown did not mean every dollar had vanished. Quibi said it intended to return remaining cash to investors; reports at the time estimated that roughly $350 million remained. In January 2021, Roku acquired Quibi's content library, giving the shows another route to an audience.

The company was finished. The content survived. But the original vision — a standalone premium streaming service built around quick bites of Hollywood entertainment — had failed.

And that left a bigger question than how quickly Quibi collapsed.

**Was this a once-in-a-generation disaster caused by a once-in-a-generation pandemic — or had the pandemic merely exposed a mistake that was already built into the business?**

*[RE-HOOK / TRANSITION: VISUAL: Quibi's logo on a black screen. The word “WHY?” appears.]*

---

# ACT VII — THE AUTOPSY

**NARRATOR — reflective, increasingly intense:**

It would be easy to blame the pandemic. And the timing certainly didn't help.

It would be easy to blame the mobile-first launch experience. That created friction, and Quibi had to respond to what users wanted.

It would also be easy to conclude that consumers simply didn't want short-form entertainment.

But that explanation misses the point.

Short-form video was already thriving. The real question was whether Quibi had created a product people wanted enough to pay for, separate from the platforms they already used.

Its founders saw a world where premium entertainment could fill every spare minute. But they built a business around the assumption that those spare minutes represented an underserved market — and that Hollywood-quality production could turn that opportunity into a subscription empire.

The company committed enormous resources before it had convincingly established that demand.

It treated production quality, celebrity involvement, and technological innovation as powerful advantages.

They were advantages.

But none of them guaranteed product-market fit.

And that is the uncomfortable lesson of Quibi.

**Money can buy the content. It can buy the talent. It can buy the technology.**

**But it cannot force people to care.**

*[BEAT.]*

And if that sounds obvious now, remember what made Quibi so compelling in the first place: its founders weren't foolish outsiders. They were experienced leaders with remarkable résumés. They had access to Hollywood, influential investors, and billions of dollars.

Which means the failure wasn't simply a story about not having enough resources.

It was a story about believing resources could compensate for uncertainty about the customer.

**And that brings us to the most expensive question Quibi left behind.**

---

# FINAL CONCLUSION — THE MOST EXPENSIVE QUESTION

*[VISUAL: A smartphone lies on a table. Its screen lights up with the Quibi logo, then fades to black.]*

**NARRATOR — slow, intimate, dark:**

Quibi wasn't an attempt to make something cheap.

It was an attempt to make something premium.

Its founders believed that if you gave people Hollywood-quality stories in ten-minute installments, they would find the time to watch them.

And maybe there was something real in that vision.

But there is a difference between predicting what people might enjoy...

...and building something they will actually choose.

Quibi had influential leaders, billions of dollars in funding, celebrities, studios, and ambitious technology. What it couldn't establish was a compelling enough reason for consumers to keep coming back.

And just over six months after launch, the experiment was over.

Perhaps Quibi's biggest mistake wasn't believing that the future of entertainment would be shorter.

Perhaps it was believing that understanding the future meant understanding the customer.

Because in business, the most dangerous assumption isn't that your idea could fail.

It's believing that your money, reputation, and confidence will somehow make it succeed.

*[LONG PAUSE. MUSIC FADES.]*

**Quibi raised $1.75 billion to build the future of entertainment.**

**It lasted just over six months.**

And the question it left behind is one every entrepreneur should ask before spending their first dollar:

*Are you building something people actually want... or something you've convinced yourself they should want?*

*[FADE TO BLACK.]*

# Production notes

- **Tone:** Dark, cinematic business documentary with restrained sarcasm. Use power words when they earn their place; don't make every sentence sound like a trailer.
- **Pacing:** The opening should grab attention in the first five seconds. Let key contrasts breathe. Avoid explaining the entire premise immediately.
- **Re-hooking:** Every act should answer the previous act's question while creating the next one. Don't repeat the same question; escalate it.
- **Visual approach:** Archival footage, headlines, production imagery, app screens, financial graphics, and deliberate contrasts between smartphones and living-room televisions.
- **Accuracy:** Verify financial figures, contemporary subscriber estimates, reported remaining cash, and quotations against reliable sources. Funding raised is not the same as money lost, and downloads are not the same as paying subscribers.
